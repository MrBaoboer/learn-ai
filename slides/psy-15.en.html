Theory in a minute · Anchors and decoys

Kahneman and Tversky showed that judgments about numbers get pulled by the number you saw first—even when that number came from a spinning wheel. That's anchoring. The decoy effect goes further: put an obviously bad deal among the options; nobody picks it, but it changes the relative attractiveness of the others. Print-only at $125 is the decoy: its only job is to make the “add $0, get digital” bundle look like a steal.

Every row on a pricing table is working—including the one nobody buys. It isn't dead stock; it's a salesperson.

Key Takeaways
✓

Price feel is all reference: Nobody knows what an AI assistant “should” cost—judgment comes from the anchors you place. The row nobody buys is still working; it's a salesperson.

✓

Three tiers is the standard structure: Starter catches the hesitant, the top tier is the anchor, mid-tier takes the bulk. The “Most popular” label is social proof—another nudge.

✓

AI versioning is natural: Model tiers (base / flagship / strongest reasoning) have real cost differences users can feel—far more decent price discrimination than “feature castration.”

✓

The red line is non-chosen identity: What users actively choose (student verification, annual pay, lower-tier) is decent; quietly repricing by user profile waits for the headline. Always give existing users a grace period on hikes.
