A file drops in the running chat: Sunday’s half-marathon handbook, 40 pages. You only need three things: bag-drop cutoff, when your wave starts, and the 15 km cutoff. It is Friday night. The race is a day and a half away.

You figure if you are racing, you should start at page 1 and read honestly. Cover, organizers, sponsors, weather notes. Then “net time,” “wave check-in,” “sweep” — you stop and search again. The order is earnest. You skip no words.

By 10:25 p.m. you are on page 8 and have stopped six times. Bag-drop cutoff is still missing. The 15 km cutoff table is on page 27. You had paced from when you would cross the start. There you finally see:

The 15 km cutoff is 2 hours 10 minutes after this wave’s gun. Time spent starting late counts toward the cutoff. Runners past cutoff must stop and take the sweep vehicle to the finish.

You are in wave C. You cross the start 8 minutes after the gun. The time left to you is 8 minutes shorter. The pace sheet you just made has to be redone. The organizer list and weather notes you already read never answered the three questions in your hand. The 45 minutes are gone too.

You start at page 1 because you treat page order as understanding order: basics first, details later. A race handbook’s job is to collect every rule. The important bits sit in the schedule, the course map, the cutoff table, and the glossary. You treated page order as importance order.

Before you start the whole file, the AI can plot the path

Dump all 40 pages and ask “summarize this,” and it usually crushes 40 pages into two. Shorter, yes. Net time and gun time still sit in the same paragraph. You still do not know which word rewrites your pace plan.

More useful: let it build the structure from contents, abstract, key conclusions, and figures, then ask which five concepts you need to read this. Quiz one by one. Cross off what you can explain. Fill only what you cannot say. The AI finds the entrance and the gaps first. The body waits until those two are clear.

  1. Read the contents first, see which sections hold the answers — bag drop in the schedule, start in the waves, cutoff in the course table.
  2. Then the abstract and key conclusions. Grab the rules that change an action.
  3. Then the figures. Put times, kilometer marks, and waves back on one map.
  4. Last, the body. Read only the passages about you and the five prerequisite concepts.

I am about to read 【material name】. From the contents, abstract, conclusions, and figures, tell me which three questions it answers, and which section holds each one. Then: which five concepts do I need first? Sort by dependency. Do not explain yet. Quiz me one by one. Cross off what I can answer. Fill what I cannot with a everyday example. One at a time. Wait until I can say it in my own words before you continue.

If the file has no abstract or conclusions, have the AI pull them from the contents, chapter openings, tables, and end summaries. Do not hunt for sections that are not there just to keep the order.

“Sort by dependency” decides the order. If net time and gun time are both unclear, learn when each clock starts before you talk about the 15 km cutoff table. Otherwise the AI explains five words in the order it thought of them, and the later explanation smuggles the earlier unknown back in.

“Do not explain yet. Quiz me one by one” is also the key. It checks what you already have. If you can say net time starts at the start mat, cross it off. If you cannot say how gun time moves the cutoff, that is where the time goes.

Five concepts are just a start list

Five is a handy start number. Enough to see structure, not so many that the first self-test drags. A narrow file may only have three. Across many chapters, list five, fill them, then ask the AI if a next layer of gaps is waiting.

Cross off what you can answer. Fill only what you cannot. That stops another waste: letting the AI start from the most basic definition, while you sit through what you already know. The check should land on a concrete question, like “wave C crosses 8 minutes after the gun — does cutoff shrink by those 8 minutes?” You only cross it off if you can say why.

If you are already stuck on one sentence, go back to the four-step unpack and open only that gap. How to confirm you can say it after you fill: the can-you-say-it gate.
