You are checking this month’s electricity bill for the family: total use is 210 kWh, and the first tier caps at 200 kWh. The AI puts the question above the answer.

Once you pass the first tier, does all 210 kWh switch to the second-tier rate, or only the extra 10 kWh?

You vaguely lean toward the second, but you don’t write it down. You feel one confirmation away, so you flick the wheel. The answer says: only the extra 10 kWh is billed at the second tier; the first 200 stay at the first. You read it for 7 seconds and jot, “Oh right. That’s what I thought.”

At dinner someone holds up the same bill and asks why it jumped the moment you passed 200 kWh. You say at once: “Cross the tier, and all 210 kWh go to the second rate.” The line items sit right there. You just said the opposite of the answer you read.

You go back for your first thought. It isn’t there. The on-screen answer already handed you a direction, the wording, and the result. Recall follows those ready-made cues. “That’s what I thought” and “that’s what I thought after I saw it” get mixed.

If you had paused one second and written the result, the rule, and when it fails, you would see which line the error sits on. The three lines are evidence from the you before the answer walked in. That evidence may be wrong. Wrong is useful — it tells you what to patch next.

Why this chance to judge only comes once

Once you have seen the answer, you can never know how much you would have thought of on your own. Every sentence after that may already have borrowed its words. The chance to check what you currently understand exists only before the answer appears.

Write it first, and the page keeps a version the answer has not edited. When it is revealed, you can check line by line: did you guess the result, name the rule, and find its range? The line that misses is the line you patch next.

Have the AI hold the answer first

The AI can write a boundary problem for you, and it can hide the answer. The key is to put the order in the request, and make it wait until you have handed in three lines.

  1. Show only the problem first. Hide the answer, the hints, and the keywords.
  2. Wait until you have written three lines. In between, only confirm receipt. Do not correct you along the way.
  3. Then compare line by line. Point out where the prediction, the rule, and the failure condition each miss.
What I think will happen: the bill is only a little higher than when it was exactly 200 kWh. Which rule I am using: each tier prices only the kWh that fall inside that band. When this rule fails: if the billing cycle or a shared-meter rule changes, the tier cutoffs move with it.

What each of the three lines is checking

Line one writes the result. It keeps the prediction you made with no hint, and turns “I kind of get it” into a sentence you can mark right or wrong.

Line two writes the rule. You can luck into the result. Naming which rule is doing the work shows whether you actually ran the calculation.

Line three writes when it fails. Every rule has a range. Change the billing cycle, a shared meter, or a local rule, and the old cutoffs may move. Line three checks whether you understand a rule, or only memorized this problem’s answer.
