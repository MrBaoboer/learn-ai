A good reading experience and knowledge landing in your head are two different jobs. Cover that smooth explanation, say three points, and the missing one shows.

“How do you tell a cold from the flu?” Someone forwards you the community flu-shot booking and tosses that in. That night, 10:40, you are not sure, so you ask the AI to say it in plain words.

It gives you four sentences. You know every word. Each step follows the last.

A cold usually eases in over a day or two, mostly in the nose and throat. The flu often hits in a few hours, with fever and body aches more likely. The flu has its own antivirals and a vaccine. A cold has neither.

You read it top to bottom, nod twice, and reply: “Got it. Mainly how fast it starts, where it hurts, and whether there is a drug and a vaccine.” The three points sit right there. The sentences are short. You really feel you have it.

Next morning, 8:15, at breakfast: “Last night it came on sudden — fever, aches all over. Cold or flu? Which one has a vaccine?” You say “probably a cold,” pause two seconds, switch to “flu, maybe.” On the vaccine line you only remember that one of them has it. You cannot say which.

The four sentences from last night have not even sat a full night, and you have to reopen the chat. That is when the gap shows: every sentence made sense while you read. Off the original, one of the three you cannot even point the right way.

Why it felt like you already had it

While you read, the answer, the order, and the hints sit in front of you. You just walk the sentences. “Hits suddenly” — nod. “Has a vaccine” — nod. The original lines keep handing you cues, so nothing stalls.

At the breakfast table the original is gone. You have to pull three comparison angles out of your head, then put cold and flu back on the right side. That is a different move. You have to retrieve the answer yourself.

Following someone else’s sentences only means you recognize the road. Walking it after it is covered means the route is yours.

The AI can turn a smooth explanation into a check

Ask “can you make it even clearer?” and you usually get another, smoother paragraph. You can nod the whole way again. A move that actually works: have the AI pause the lecture and cover what it just said.

  1. First split out three checkable questions. In this material they ask how fast it starts, where it hurts, and about the drug and the vaccine.
  2. Show one question at a time. Before you answer, it does not restate the original, and it does not hand you keywords.
  3. After you answer, open and compare. If a direction is flipped or a line is missing, reread only that one.

The AI’s job shifts from making it read smoothly to hiding the answer and holding the self-check. After it is covered, how many lines can you still say? The demo below starts from that question.

The full explanation the AI gave you

Cold and flu differ by a word, and they are two things caused by two kinds of virus.

A cold usually builds over a day or two: dry throat first, then a stuffy nose. The misery sits in the nose and throat.

The flu often drops in within a few hours: fever and body aches first. A stuffy nose is oddly mild.

Also, the flu has its own antivirals — the earlier, the more they help — and a yearly vaccine. A cold has neither a dedicated drug nor a vaccine.

Paste these three sentences straight to the AI

After you finish an explanation, send the next three sentences in order. As many core points as the material has, that many questions. We use three here because that passage compared three things.

Copy the whole block
  1. Generate the self-check questions firstSplit the explanation you just gave into three questions I can mark right or wrong. Each question checks only one point. No answers yet.
  2. Then hide the answersShow one question at a time. Do not hint before I answer, and do not restate the original. After I answer, show the next.
  3. Finally, check only the gapsAfter all three, compare them one by one with the original. Tell me which line flipped direction, which one I missed, and have me reread only the one I got wrong.

The key in sentence two is “do not hint before I answer.” Drop that half-sentence and the AI will tack on an explanation after the question. You are back to nodding at the answer.

Self-check has to happen when the original is out of sight

Close the page, or minimize the window, then say the three points in your own words. Stall halfway and you have found the spot. Go back to only the missing sentence. Read it, cover it, say it again.

This is a self-check you can actually do. It does not ask you to judge whether you “understand.” It checks one visible result: after the original is gone, can you still say the key parts back.
