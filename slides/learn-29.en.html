When you close the video, your fingers still know where to go next. C, G, Am — you drilled them for 90 minutes, and by the end you could switch eight times in a row without a pause. That was Monday night.

You glance at the next six weeks on the syllabus and write this:

These three chords feel solid already. I will find time to review this week.

Tuesday, sudden overtime; you only shower after getting home. Wednesday's new video starts strumming; you practice an hour. Thursday the group chat sends a chorus tutorial; you follow for forty minutes. Friday night, dinner out. Sunday, the calendar has 45 minutes left. You spend them on the next lesson, telling yourself review can wait until next week.

The next Monday, the video wants C, G, Am plus strumming. C is fine. Switching to G you stop three times. Am you have to look down to find. Of the three chords you switched in a row last week, only one still lands on the first try.

The judgment at the time was not strange. Right after practice, the moves, the picture, and the voiceover are still in front of you, so “I can follow along now” slides into “I can still do it next week.” One more layer: new lessons come with a clear title and a runtime; review is only the phrase “find time.” Every day you re-plan, and new material always gets the slot first.

Which step can the AI take first

If you only tell the AI “make me a six-week practice plan,” it will usually line up new material neatly, then tack a review line onto each weekend. By Wednesday, when time runs short, the first thing cut is still that review with no object and no fixed length.

Change how you brief it, and the AI can budget the time for you:

  1. Count the week's total hours first, using only the hours the calendar will actually give you.
  2. Lock the hours review will take, and write which move you will redo and for how many minutes.
  3. Then schedule this week's new material, and push anything that will not fit to a later week, in writing.

Planned this way, “find time this week” becomes a visible cell on the calendar, and deferred new lessons stay in sight.

Where the two plans actually differ

Choose “new material first,” and week three's new lesson wants 5 hours — the whole week. Review sits at the end and gets the leftover 0. Week four wants 6 hours, and the plan again hands every hour to what is in front of you. Nobody deleted review. It never got a slot in the first place.

Choose “lock review first,” and each week first takes 2 hours, written as “no video — replay the last two weeks of chords and strumming.” The remaining 3 hours take new lessons. The hour that will not fit shows up under “push later,” and the cost is visible: new material moves a little slower, but the earlier moves stay usable.

Review only stops competing every day once it is already in the time budget.

The key half-sentence is “list the deferred new material in the open.” Without it, the AI will gladly stuff a 7-hour job into 5 hours, then hide the overflow behind a table that looks complete. Write the deferred items, and the cost of not enough time stays on the plan.

Review items must be actions too. “Review lesson one” has no finish line. “Close the video and switch the three chords eight times in a row” does.
