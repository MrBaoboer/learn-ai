Let AI Confirm Which Version I Have First

First lock down the source, date, and version clues of the file in your hands. After that, every judgment sits on this edition only.

In one sentence

The same file name can hide two different documents. Confirm this copy’s date and source first. After that, every judgment only trusts this edition.

Your papers were sent back. The clerk pushed the clear folder across the counter and drew a line under item 6: the bank statements had no stamps. Not today.

Ten days earlier you opened the checklist on the visa center site and prepared 9 items from it. HR wrote the employment letter, you reshot the photos, and printed the hotel booking twice. The file came from the official page. Every box was ticked. You had no reason to hunt for another copy.

At 9:10 on appointment morning you reached the window. The clerk said the list had been updated three days ago: statements now had to cover the last 6 months, stamped on every page. You brought 3 months. That sentence on the page had already changed.

The next slot was 11 days later. Your flight was in 18 days. In the hallway you opened the PDF on your phone, then the official site. Both files were named “Personal tourist visa checklist.pdf.” The old file had no publish date. The site popped up no warning that it had changed.

Your call felt natural: a file from the official site must be the current rule. The miss happened one step earlier. You confirmed the source, not which edition it was. The same file name can cover an update. Bookmarks and download history will not flag the change for you.

If you toss the old PDF at AI and ask “what am I still missing,” it can only keep checking along the old list. A steadier move: give it the old file and today’s official page together. Have it find dates, sources, and edits first. When the version is unclear, it stops — no filling in guesses. Then ask: which edition was I actually using, and when did item 6 change?

Why locking the version first makes later judgments stand

AI is good at walking through a document. It cannot know, from thin air, how many times the page changed after you downloaded it. With only an undated PDF, the most it can say is “version cannot be confirmed.” Ask it to guess a publish date and you get a story that merely sounds complete.

Version info has to answer at least four questions:

  1. What this document is called, so same-named files do not get mixed.
  2. Where you got it, keep the official page or the notice it came from.
  3. What date the page shows, if there is none, write “cannot confirm” as-is.
  4. When you saved it, so later comparisons have a time boundary.

Once those four are confirmed, AI can split “what the old list said then” from “what the current page says now.” Lock the edition this session is based on first. Hang every citation and conclusion under it.

Send this paragraph with your documents to AI

Before you keep comparing or decide, pin the basis down. Copy the block below as a whole. Swap the bracketed bits for your documents.

Copy this as-is I will provide [the old file] and [today’s official page]. Do not judge what I should prepare yet. First list, for each: document name, source, publish and update dates shown on the page or file, when I saved or visited it, and any other version clues. Where you cannot confirm, write “version cannot be confirmed.” Do not guess. Then compare additions, deletions, and rewrites line by line. After I confirm “the basis for this session,” later answers use only that edition. Every time you cite a rule, name which edition it came from.

If you only have one file, start with this paragraph anyway. AI will split what it can and cannot confirm. It cannot rebuild an old page you never gave it.

The first line — do not judge what I should prepare yet — is the hinge. It holds the answer at version confirmation. Without that half-sentence, AI will likely guess the edition and hand you a packing list in the same breath. A complete list makes it easy to forget the basis was never confirmed.

Keep this line too: if you cannot confirm it, say so plainly. Missing version info is itself a result. That sentence sends you back to the official site for an update date, to a phone call, or to saving today’s page — before you decide.

Which documents are worth this step first

Any rules page that can cost you money, a booking, or a submission is worth locking first. Expense policies, admissions notices, ticket-booking rules, and app terms often overwrite the old page in place. Save a PDF, then note the source link and save time. Only then is there something to compare later.

If the file prints a publish date, copy it. If the page has none, write “page undated, saved on [day].” That note does not prove when the site updated. It does prove your copy already existed by that day.

Name the edition first. Then trust judgments built on it.
