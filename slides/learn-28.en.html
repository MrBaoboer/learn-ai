On the day a milestone is due, you hand over something other people can see. Before you start, run a pre-check.

The photography course bar is already at 68%. Exposure, composition, portrait light — all three chapters checked, and you did watch each video to the end. Looking at that row of green ticks, you feel you have crossed beginner.

A friend is throwing a birthday at home and asks if you can shoot a few frames. You say yes, and even switch the camera off Auto. Aperture, shutter, ISO — you recognize every word from class. Standing by the window, you cannot tell which one to move first.

You twist a few numbers from memory. After 27 frames, faces by the window go black, then blow out; anyone moves and the shot blurs. Forty minutes later you flip back to Auto. Course progress did not roll back. The three ticks are still there.

You had treated “watched” as “can use,” because the platform logs a completion every time a lesson ends. It never asked you to set parameters alone, or to say why. A progress percent only records how many pages you walked. It cannot hand over what you can already do.

So how should next week be written, so Sunday shows whether you can do it? And how do you find out before you start that you still do not have f-stops, instead of locking up at the party? Hold those two questions and rewrite the three plans below.

Why the progress bar always looks fine

“Finish chapter three this week” has an easy verb: finish watching. The video hits the end and the task is done. On Sunday you can only confirm your eyes touched the content, not what your hands can still do after the video is gone.

Turn the milestone into a piece you can hand over, and the check has a place to land. This week in photography: three shots that only change shutter. Next week: composition sketches with the subject marked. The week after: portraits under three lamp directions. Photos, sketches, and a non-photographer's call can all be laid out and checked.

When you write it, put the date, the piece, and the pass line in one sentence. That opening week can read:

By Sunday night I will hand over three photos of the same window plant, changing only shutter, so they run dark, normal, bright. All three exist, and a friend can rank them by brightness without a caption — that is done.

When a milestone is due, look at the table for a finished piece first, then talk percent. If one sentence cannot name what you will hand over, the step is usually still too big. Split it until it fits a week and someone else can check it.

Before you shoot, find what the course assumes you already know

Once the piece is clear, do not rush into lesson one. Tell the AI the three photos you will hand over, ask it to list the concepts you need first, then one question at a time. You can paste this:

I need to shoot, on my own, three photos of the same scene: dark, normal, bright. List the basics I need before I shoot. Ask one everyday question at a time. If I miss, name the missing concept and give a ten-minute drill. Do not go on to later material.

It asks first: “Same scene — which opens the lens more, f/2.8 or f/16?” You cannot answer, so the gap is f-stop. Then you shoot the same plant at f/2.8 and f/8, compare brightness and blur, and only then go back to the three shutter shots. Input: the three photos you will hand over. Check: f-stop failed. After the drill: two photos you can compare. Every step leaves something to look at.
