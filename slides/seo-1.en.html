You Built It. Why Isn't Anyone Coming?

Earlier lessons got the product built. OPC locked down the rights. Then comes a plainer question: shipping is not the same as being found. This lesson takes "discovery" apart: which three roads traffic comes from, what each one is like, then a five-layer funnel to find where your product is stuck.

In one sentence

Three traffic doors, three temperaments: search engines when people look, AI engines when people ask, social when someone else talks about you. Run the indexing funnel first and see which layer your product is stuck on

More stuff. Same doors.

Vibe Coding drove the cost of shipping down to almost nothing. The less-mentioned flip side: if you can ship a product in ten minutes, so can everyone else. What gets built is multiplying in plain sight. The doors to user attention are still the same few. Competition didn't vanish — it just moved from "can you build it" to "will anyone see it."

A lot of indie hackers follow this path: grind for a month, ship, post it on WeChat Moments (or Twitter), get thirty visitors, then zero a week later. Conclusion: "the product is bad." Most of the time that verdict is early. The product may be fine. The problem is it never entered any discovery channel at all.

Shipping is only the start. Being found is what counts as launch. A product nobody visits and a product that was never built look the same to the market.

Search engines

Users show up with a question. Open it and read its temperament.

Who drives itThe user. They type a question. Your page has to be the answer
Time to workSlow. Indexing takes weeks, ranking takes months — but once it lands, people keep coming
What you controlContent and structure are entirely in your hands. Of the three, this is the one you control most
AI engines

Users stopped searching. They just ask. Whether you're in the answer is the AI's call.

Who drives itThe AI. It reads the web for the user, then decides whom to cite. Users see an answer. What you're fighting for is one citation inside it
Time to workDepends on whether you made it into its index and retrieval sources. Once you're in, you can get cited
What you controlSome of it. You can improve how you write (all of Lesson 3). Whether it cites you is still its decision
Social spread

Someone else talks about you. It arrives fast. It leaves fast.

Who drives itThe platform and the people who share. Whether a post takes off — you can influence it, you can't decide it
Time to workFast. You'll see the curve the same day. It usually falls back within a week
What you controlThe least. It's a spark, not a foundation
All three open — the division of labor is clear. Social spread lights the fuse. Search engines are the long game. AI engines are the new door still growing up. The rest of this Part only covers the last two, because they share one thing: slow to work, but you can control them, and doing it right once lasts. Try it · The indexing funnel: which layer are you leaking?

"Nobody's coming" is a blurry complaint. Split it into five layers: you built it, it can be crawled, it got indexed, it got shown, it got clicked. Each layer leaks a batch. The four switches below map to four common diseases. Try turning them off, then on, and watch the numbers on each layer change.

Indexing funnel simulator Four switches
Switches start all off — that's the real state of a lot of new sites at launch. Flip them on one by one and watch which layer gets fixed
Body text visible on the serverThe body is already in the page source. Crawlers can read it without running JS. The favorite mistake of AI-generated frontends: render everything in JS
Submitted sitemap and webmaster toolsTell the engines "I'm here, and here are my pages." Indexing stops being luck and becomes something you can measure
Every page has a human title and descriptionThose two lines are what users see on the results page. Write them like a person or nobody clicks
The content answers a question people searchHowever many pages you have — if nobody searches the question they answer, you'll never get shown
Bar values are illustrative indexes — they show relative loss at each layer, not real stats.

After playing it you should see a pattern: the four switches each fix a different layer. None of them can stand in for another. Visible body text fixes "can be crawled." sitemap fixes "got indexed." title fixes "got clicked." Topic choice fixes "got shown." Before you complain about traffic, find which layer you're leaking. Later lessons prescribe by layer.

Try it · Three minutes: is your product even there?

No tools to install. Three moves give your site a rough diagnosis. If you have a product now, do it as you read. If not, try it on some small tool site you use. Tap an item when you've done it.

✓ Ask an AI a problem your product can solve Use your target user's own words. Ask ChatGPT or Kimi. See if you're in the answer — and if your competitors are This step diagnoses "do AI engines see you?" If the answer is all competitors, someone is already harvesting this door and you're not at the table. If nobody is in the answer, the question is still open — that's an opportunity.
✓ Search your own brand name Add "what is" or "review" after the product name. Is page one you — or someone else introducing you? This step diagnoses "are you holding your brand terms?" If you can't find yourself by your own name, users who heard of you still can't reach you. That's the unluckiest break in the discovery chain.

It's 2026. Why still learn SEO?

A fair objection: users went off to ask AI. Is search-engine optimization a dead craft? Split it open and the opposite is true. First, search isn't dead: a lot of "find this site" demand still goes through the search box, especially with transactional intent (prices, tools, comparing services). Second, and more important: before an AI engine answers, it has to "search" too. Its retrieval sources are search-engine indexes and the content on your site. The SEO basics — crawlable, readable — are exactly the ticket to being cited by AI.
