Solo Priorities: Write Questions People Actually Search

The first three lessons covered the technical checklist — all one-time jobs. The real daily fight is content: what to write, and what to write first. A one-person company has no SEO team and no keyword-tool budget. Your only weapon is ranking your bets: spend effort on questions people search, trade real experience for trust, and stay off the crooked path.

Write the question first, then add features

Indie hackers' content habit is a "feature lens": changelogs, feature roundups, version comparisons — written diligently, searched by nobody. Users don't search "what's new in XX tool v2.3." They search their own trouble: "special vs regular VAT invoice" or "cat vaccine expired, what now." First rule of topic selection: write the sentence users will actually type into a search box or an AI chat.

A good topic has two dimensions: intent strength (how close is the searcher to using your product) and competition (how many people already answered this well). Strong intent + weak competition is a gold mine. One-person companies dig those. Leave the fat-head keywords to the big players.

Trade real experience for trust

Topic selection answers "what to write." What's left is "why you." Search engines and AI engines have been tightening the same valve for years: they prefer first-hand experience. Google wrote Experience into its content rater guidelines. When AI engines pick citations, they clearly favor paragraphs with concrete numbers and lived detail — you saw that in last lesson's citation auction.

That's huge news for a one-person company: you have no editorial team, but you have something nobody can copy. Real bills, real error logs, real negotiation notes, real holes you fell into. This site's OPC part used our own trademark rejection as teaching material and a real domain-negotiation quote as a case — those pages are among the most cited. Write what you actually did, with the real numbers from the process. That's a fingerprint AI mass-spam pages can never fake.

Inventory what you may already have. Each item is a piece nobody else can write:

  • A real cloud bill: "what does an XX-class product actually cost per month" is always searched.
  • A full incident timeline: error screenshots, the debugging path, the one line that was the culprit.
  • Negotiation notes with vendors, platforms, or clients (after redaction): price questions have extremely strong intent.
  • Rejection stories: trademark refused, listing rejected, loan denied. Few people write "failure." Many people search it.
  • Bad decisions and rework logs: anything that starts with "if only I had…" is naturally credible.

For redaction, follow this site's rule: keep amounts, dates, and process (that's where trust comes from); hide counterpart names, document numbers, and personal information.

Write new, or go back and update the old

Once the tracker is running, you'll face a weekly micro-decision: does this week's slot go to a new topic, or to rewriting an old article? The rule is simpler than it sounds:

SituationActionWhy
Old page ranks, but the data is staleUpdate firstAdding fresh data to a page that already ranks is the highest-ROI move of all: the foundation is there, you just add a layer
Old page has no rank and no clicksDiagnose, then decideGo back to lesson 1's funnel and find which layer leaks. If nobody searches the topic, drop it without regret. If the title is the drag, two lines of copy will do
The tracker has a strong-intent, weak-competition candidateWrite newGold-mine topics expire. If someone else answers first, that competition cell changes

Rough rhythm: for every two or three new pieces, go back and refresh one old one. When you update, also update dateModified — lesson 3 covered this. AI engines really do look at that date.

Where AI sits in content production

By this point in the course, you'll probably think: just let AI write the content too. The answer depends on the job. AI is a great assistant and a terrible printing press: use it to speed up your real experience and you double output; use it to mass-produce pages from thin air and you slide into the spam pages on the anti-list below.

StageLet AI do it (assistant seat)Don't let AI do it (press seat)
TopicsTranslate one feature into twenty candidate questions; you pickLet it decide what to write — it will only pick fat-head keywords
DraftTurn your voice notes, logs, and bills into a structured first draftInvent a "deep, quality piece" with no source material
FactsList the numbers and sources you still need to checkLet it fill in the numbers — it will invent them with a straight face
BatchDraft one-sentence summaries for hundreds of pages; you review, then shipGenerate hundreds of standalone pages nobody checked

The line is really one sentence: does the page contain something only you can provide? If yes, AI can help as much as it wants and it's still your content. If no, even if you typed it by hand, it's spam. This site's "in one sentence" blurbs are "batch-draft, then human-review" — hundreds of pages, one person, two days.

After all six, the pattern is one line: anything that tries to fool the engine is a trap; anything that helps the engine read real content is safe. A one-person company can't afford a whole-site penalty. Don't test that line.

When a hustler shows up, the pitch is always three lines: "internal channel," "guaranteed ranking," "do nothing and competitors will pass you." Hear any one of them and the conversation is over. The money you save will renew every TLD variant for three years.

Key takeaways
✓

Write the question first, then add features: users search their own trouble, not your changelog. Run every topic through two meters — intent strength and competition — and dig only the strong-intent, weak-competition gold mines.

✓

One feature, five questions: if you can't write five sentences in the user's own words, suspect a fake need. The questions themselves are your content calendar.

✓

Real experience is a fingerprint nobody can copy: bills, errors, negotiation notes, holes you fell into. Engines and AI are both weighting first-hand experience. That's a one-person company's only moat against content mills — and the deepest one.

✓

Black-hat in one sentence: fooling the engine is a trap; helping it read real content is safe. Vendors who promise rankings go straight on the blocklist.
