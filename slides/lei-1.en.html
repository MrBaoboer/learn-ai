Who Should Start a Company: That Irrational Confidence

Who is cut out for entrepreneurship? Lei Jun's answer starts with a joke: 「Starting a business is not something for normal people — it's for the crazy ones.」 This lesson covers the three capabilities every founder needs, and what kept him going when Kingsoft was on the brink in 1996.

A Cold-Water Opening

“Starting a company is not something for normal people — it's for the crazy ones. So most people, frankly, had better not.” Behind this joke is Lei Jun's real conviction: what matters most is not capability, but whether you have the resolve and courage to do it. Without those two things, nothing else matters.

Three Capabilities of a Founder

After resolve, Lei Jun listed three essential capabilities — and specifically emphasized: the hardest of the three is the last one.

01

Courage to Face Adversity

Setbacks are the norm in entrepreneurship. Getting beaten by competitors, losing teammates, running out of money — all of this will happen. Courage is a consumable resource.

02

Ability to Paint a Vision

You need to be able to articulate clearly where you're going and why it matters. The vision is not a pitch deck for investors — it's the only fuel your team has when things get hard.

03

Ability to Rally People Behind a Dream

In Lei Jun's own words: 「The hardest part is whether you can gather a big group of people to pursue your dream and ambition.」 You believing alone is not enough — you need a crowd to believe with you.

Kingsoft's First Crisis

In its early days, Kingsoft developed WPS — a product that was extremely well-suited to the market and well-loved by users. 「Kingsoft became a success overnight.」 Then Microsoft entered China: their software was unencrypted, and piracy spread it at incredible speed.

Soon, Kingsoft found itself squeezed between Microsoft and rampant piracy. Lei Jun said the hardest things in that crisis were two: convincing yourself it was still worth fighting for, and convincing every colleague that there was still hope.

The Negative Spiral: When Nobody Believes

Convincing yourself is only step one. Step two is convincing everyone around you. Lei Jun described the negative spiral he personally lived through:

Today, this person quit. Core team members start wavering — with perfectly valid reasons.
Tomorrow, another strong player leaves. The remaining people start checking in with each other nervously.
The company enters a negative spiral. “You start to feel like you might close the doors tomorrow.”
Nobody in the world believes anymore. “It's pretty devastating.” Almost every company in crisis goes through this.

“Entrepreneurship actually requires faith. You must first believe this thing can happen — only then do you have enough confidence to convince the people working alongside you.” Lei Jun called this “irrational confidence”: if you're not confident, there's absolutely no chance. Faith isn't a performance for outsiders — it's the only thing that stops the spiral from accelerating.

OPC Perspective
Negative Spiral · Solo Founder Edition

A solo company has no colleagues to convince — the entire negative spiral happens inside your own head. Kingsoft's spiral was key people quitting one after another; the indie developer version is: three months after launch with no paying users → start doubting the direction → update frequency drops → metrics get worse.

Lei Jun's solution applies equally: convince yourself first that this is worth doing — and do it when the metrics look worst.

Three Capabilities · AI Era Edition
  • Organizing Capability: 「Rallying a group of people」 now means orchestrating a team of Agents: translate the vision into clear PRDs and rules so that Cursor, contractors, and collaborators all move in the same direction even without your constant supervision.
  • Irrational Confidence: Kingsoft vs. Microsoft was a battle logic said was unwinnable. A solo founder vs. a well-staffed competitor is the same. Logic helps you choose the battlefield — but what keeps you on it has never been logic.
