“Twenty years later it's that much more because… because it grows exponentially.” You stop there.

Saturday, 4 p.m. You're at the table explaining compound interest to your eighth-grade niece. Ten minutes ago you finished a six-minute explainer. You can recite the definition, and on paper you got 100 becoming 105 in year one, 110.25 in year two. Both numbers are right. You feel like you've got it.

She asks first: “Who pays the extra twenty-five cents in year two?” You point at the first year's 5 and say it stays on the account and earns interest too. That one goes smoothly.

Then she slides the calculator over. At 5% a year: about 163 after 10 years, about 265 after 20. She asks: “Why did the second decade add 102, and the first only 63?” You say that half-sentence from a moment ago.

“Compound interest is interest on interest. Principal and old interest make new interest together, so the longer it runs, the stronger it gets — because that's exponential growth.”

She follows at once: “What does exponential growth mean? Why does it make the second decade grow faster?” You reopen the article. Your last two sentences are almost the article's own words. The real question still has no answer. You can recite the definition and compute two years from the example — that's why you judged yourself done.

Why you stall even after you can recite the definition

Reciting uses the sentence you just stored. The article said “the longer it runs, the stronger compound interest gets,” and you can say the same. As long as the original words are still there, the sentence keeps going.

Explaining is another job: you have to know which process each word points to. “Exponential growth” here has to land on that larger total each year: year two's 5% grows from 105, year three from 110.25. Each year's starting line already carries what grew before. If one word only fetches another word, the listener still doesn't get the process.

Reciting means you remembered the sentence. Explaining means you know what each word in it points to.

The AI can hold the question it doesn't get

A real listener often nods with your tone, or feels rude interrupting. The AI can stay inside the boundary you set, chase one unheard bit at a time, and point at the term you tossed back — as-is.

  1. Let it listen with only junior-high knowledge, and press down the background both sides would usually share. “Exponential growth” cannot count as a ready-made answer.
  2. Have it interrupt the moment it doesn't get it, so the gap lands on the sentence you just said — not piled up into a vague “please explain that again.”
  3. Ban it from finishing your sentence. A fluent explanation has to come from you. If it ghostwrites, you're checking whether it can talk, not you.

What each of these three limits is blocking
  1. Junior-high knowledge only blocks a default background that's too rich. The AI knows “exponential growth” and the compound-interest formula. If you don't press the line down, it walks right over the layer you never explained.
  2. Interrupt when it doesn't get it blocks questions stacking up. A follow-up after every sentence shows you which word the gap starts at.
  3. No finishing your sentence blocks the AI polishing a half-sentence into a full answer. It can flag that “exponential growth” is still unexplained. The year-by-year thickening has to come from you.
